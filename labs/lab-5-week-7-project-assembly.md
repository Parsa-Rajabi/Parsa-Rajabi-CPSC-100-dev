## What To Work On

There's no required order today, and no fixed time budget for any one piece. Use your open time, before and after the clicker, on whatever the checklist above showed is still missing, in whatever sequence makes sense for your group.

If you're behind on more than one thing: most groups find Part C's analysis sections take longer to get right than assembly does, since assembly moves fast once the individual parts already exist. That's a pattern from past terms, not a rule, so weight your own time by what your checklist actually shows, not by this paragraph.

### If Part C needs work

Sections 1 through 5 of your template are description. Sections 6 through 8 are analysis, and they're consistently where groups get stuck, not because they take longer to write, but because they ask you to argue something rather than describe it.

**Name the mechanism (template section 7)**

Pick the course concept your case turns on:

- Decision tree / classification
- Weighted scoring and thresholds
- Feedback loop
- Training-data gap
- Proxy variable: something measured standing in for something else

Write two or three sentences connecting it to *your* case, using specifics from sections 2 to 4 of your template.

> #### What "naming the mechanism" means
>
> Weak: *"The algorithm was biased against certain neighbourhoods."*
>
> Strong: *"This is a feedback loop. The system's own output (fewer drivers dispatched) reduced the ride requests coming from that area, which the system then read as confirmation of low demand."*
>
> The second one explains how the harm sustained itself. That gap, between naming a mechanism and just describing an outcome, is the most common thing groups leave unfinished in section 7.

**Argue the other reading (template section 6)**

- You've picked one of Benjamin's four categories. Now build the case for a **different** one.
- **Split your group.** Half argue the category you chose, half argue an alternative. Five minutes, out loud.
- Write down the strongest point the other side made, and put it in section 6.

> #### Why this is worth the time
>
> A case with only one defensible reading is usually a case nobody looked at hard enough. Clearview AI reads as coded exposure, obviously, given the surveillance framing. But it also reads as default discrimination: the RCMP never checked whether the data was legally collected. Both are arguable, and a report that only acknowledges the obvious one is thinner for it.

### If assembly needs work

- **Combine into one document**, Parts A, B, C in that order.
- **Label every Part A reflection with its author's name.** These are graded individually, so an unattributed reflection can't be matched to anyone.
- **Write your AI usage disclosure.** What tools, for which parts, and what was entirely your own. A short paragraph is enough.
- **Each person writes their own contribution statement.** A few sentences: what you actually did.
- **Read it once, start to finish, as one document.** Two things to catch: a claim in Part B that contradicts the results table, and any place the three parts use different language for the same idea.
- **Export as one PDF.**

> ### ⚠️ Check your sources are real: this is the last chance
>
> **Open every source in your Part C bibliography.** Confirm it exists, and confirm it says what you say it says. If a source came out of an AI tool and nobody on your team has opened it, open it now.
>
> **What counts as fabrication:**
>
> - Citing a source that doesn't exist
> - Reporting results you didn't obtain
> - Describing events that didn't happen
> - **Misrepresenting what a real source says**: you're responsible for reporting accurately what's actually in the article. Getting it wrong is not a lesser offence than making it up.
>
> Wrong page numbers, broken links, and citation formatting mistakes are errors, not fabrication.
>
> **The penalty:**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | The student responsible | **Zero on the entire project** |
> | The rest of the group | **Zero on the affected part**: Part B or Part C |
>
> Your individual Part A is unaffected.
>
> Ten minutes of checking now is worth more than anything else you'll do in this hour. Split the bibliography across your group and open every link.

> ### Your Part B start is already on record
>
> Your TAs circulated throughout Week 6's lab, and each of you handed in a postcard naming the tool you tested and what you did that day. That's deliberate: it means your results have a dated starting point, even though nobody watched every image get tested.
>
> If your final results table doesn't resemble what your group was actually doing in Week 6, that's a problem. If something legitimately changed since (you re-ran a tool, dropped an item, hit a paywall) say so in your write-up. Explained changes are fine. Unexplained ones aren't.

### If you're in good shape on both

Trade drafts with another group and read theirs cold. You'll spot missing attribution and unexplained jargon in someone else's document far faster than in your own. Or start the source-verification check above early, rather than rushing it in the last ten minutes.

## Notes for Development: REMOVE BEFORE DISTRIBUTING

### TA prep required

- **Checkoff is diagnostic, not evaluative, and this needed to be explicit.** TAs are checking that a group has a real draft and can talk through their mechanism and alternative reading, not assigning a grading band (Developing/Meets/Exceeds/Exceptional). That happens later, separately, at marking. Reworded every checkoff-adjacent line in the student-facing text to say "on track" rather than anything that could read as in-the-room grading. Worth being explicit with TAs about this distinction too, since it's an easy line to blur, especially for a TA who will also be marking this cohort later.
- Read the Part C template so you can assess a mechanism claim in thirty seconds.
- Know the difference between a weak and strong mechanism statement (the box in the "If Part C needs work" section is the script), but use it for coaching in the moment, not for privately deciding a grade. That's the marker's job, later, with the rubric in hand.
- The clicker at minute 25 has no correct answer. Use the room's response distribution to prioritize circulation for the second work block: groups answering D deserve a visit before groups answering A. Individual answers shouldn't be visible to groupmates or attributed by name when displayed, same as the existing content clickers in other labs.
- **Checkoff plan:** eight minutes, and the useful part is hearing the mechanism out loud. If time is short, cut the draft-completeness glance short and keep the conversation.

### Where this is likely to break

- **Groups arriving with nothing drafted.** The lab collapses into a writing session and the TA check-in, the actual value here, never happens. The Before Lab box is deliberately blunt.
- **A genuinely open work block can make an already-behind group freeze**, with nobody sure where to start. Mitigation: the two named clusters and the soft ratio guidance both still give a concrete default. Worth a TA saying "start with whichever of these two your checklist showed first" out loud if a group looks stuck rather than working.
- **⚠️ Fabricated sources.** The source-verification box is the last checkpoint before a zero. TAs should treat "have you opened every source?" as a real question, not a formality.

  **TAs must know the policy precisely**, because students will ask: the responsible student zeroes the whole project; the rest of the group zeroes only the affected part; individual Part A is unaffected. Misrepresenting a real source counts; a wrong page number doesn't.

  **The judgment call TAs will face:** misrepresentation vs. a defensible reading of an ambiguous source. Students find regulator reports and court filings genuinely hard to parse. Working line: *if a reasonable reader could arrive at the student's reading, it's an error, not fabrication.* Escalate anything past that to the instructor rather than deciding in the room.
- **Uneven contribution surfacing late.** Some groups will reveal a problem today, which is past the project guide's stated 12-day and 9-day intervention windows. TAs need a line for this: realistically it becomes a peer-evaluation matter rather than an intervention.

Worth deciding before this runs (see Open Questions).
