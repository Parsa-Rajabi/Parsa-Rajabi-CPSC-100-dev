## Write Your Cards

You're writing **two cards about yourself**. Same content on both. Completely different form.

> ### Markdown
>
> **Markdown** is a *markup language* — a way of adding formatting to plain text using ordinary characters, rather than clicking buttons in a word processor. You write `# Title` and something else turns it into a big bold heading.
>
> That "something else" matters. Markdown doesn't *do* anything on its own. It's a set of instructions about how text should look, and a separate program does the looking-like-it. That's what makes it a markup language and not a programming language.

### Card A — the Markdown

Write your card in **raw Markdown syntax**, exactly as you'd type it. It must have:

- **One level-1 heading** — `# Like this`
- **Two level-2 headings** — `## Like this`
- **Two bulleted items** — `- Like this`

Make it about you. Your level-1 heading should be your name. For the two level-2 headings, pick whatever you like — here are some that work:

| Pick two of these | (or make up your own) |
|---|---|
| Favourite fruit | Why I took this course |
| Favourite colour | Year at UBC |
| Lucky number | Favourite song |
| Worst number | Something I'm bad at |

2. Write Card A now. Don't rush it — someone else has to read this.

### Card B — the preview

Now write the **same card again**, but this time as it would *look* after it's rendered. No hash symbols, no dashes. A heading should look like a heading.

3. Make your level-1 heading the biggest thing on the card.
4. Make your level-2 headings smaller than that, but clearly headings — bigger or bolder or underlined.
5. Give your bullets actual bullet points.
6. **Put your name on the back of Card B**, small.

> ### Why two cards?
>
> Card A is what you *write*. Card B is what everyone else *sees*. Being able to move between those two in your head — looking at syntax and knowing what it'll produce — is most of what Markdown fluency actually is.
>
> It's also on the quiz.

**Card A goes to your TA. Card B stays with you.**

## The Hunt

You're holding raw Markdown written by someone in your half of the room. Your job:

9. **Read it and render it in your head.** What are the headings? What are the bullets? Who is this?
10. **Go find them.** Introduce yourself. Say your name before you say "is this you?"
11. When you find them, **ask to see their Card B** — that's their own preview of the card you just rendered. Compare it to what you pictured.

> ### If your version and theirs don't match
>
> Good. That means one of you rendered something differently, and figuring out which of you is right is the most useful two minutes of this lab. Common culprits: how many `#` symbols make which size of heading, and whether a `-` needs a space after it.

12. Once you've found each other, **stay together** — you'll be talking to this person again in about ten minutes.

### If You Finish Early

Swap cards with another pair and try theirs. Or write a third card using something we haven't covered — bold, italics, a link — and see if anyone can render it.

## Still Stuck?

- **"I don't know how many `#` to use."** One `#` is the biggest heading. Two `##` is the next size down. More hashes, smaller heading.
- **"My bullets aren't working."** A `-` needs a space after it before the text. `- Mango` works; `-Mango` doesn't.
- **"I can't find the person whose card I have."** They might be in the other half of the room by mistake, or absent. Tell your TA and swap for a different card.
- **Still stuck?** Flag your TA. That's what they're there for.

## Notes for Development — REMOVE BEFORE DISTRIBUTING

**Learning outcomes covered**

From `100 Learning Outcomes UPDATED` → Introduction:

- Define what a markup language is and distinguish it from programming and scripting languages → the *Markdown* definition box
- Explain the purpose of Markdown and why it's introduced early → Think It Over Q3
- Write basic Markdown syntax → Card A
- **Predict the rendered HTML output for a given piece of Markdown syntax** → Card B, the hunt, and the clicker. This is the hardest of the four and the whole lab is built to carry it.

**What students walk in knowing**

Lectures 1 (Intro, incl. Markdown), 2 and 3 (Data representation), 4 (Data storage). **No Snap yet** — Snap 1 is Friday of this week, two days after lab. Nothing here requires a computer.

**Completion model**

No Deliverables, no checkoff. Completion is by presence, recorded via the clicker at 0:34. This is the only lab in the term with no TA checkoff.

**TA prep required**

- Print or cut card stock — **two per student**, plus ~10% spares
- Decide before lab: blank cards, or pre-printed with the requirements on the back? Pre-printed kills a lot of "how many bullets again?" interruptions and costs nothing. **Open decision.**
- Rehearse the split: the room halves for the shuffle, and cards must not cross halves. Physically separating the two piles is the whole trick.
- Hold back 2–3 spare Card As for students whose target is absent.
- Read the Project 1 overview yourself — you're presenting it for five minutes.

**Where this is likely to break**

- **Timing risk: the hunt.** 12 minutes is generous when it works and not nearly enough when three people can't find their author. Watch the room at minute 28; if a handful are still wandering, call it and move on — the hunt has done its job once most people have met someone.
- **Card B quality.** The failure mode is a student who just rewrites the text with no visual difference between heading levels, which demonstrates nothing. Step 6–8 exist to force it, but TAs should spot-check during the writing block, not after. **Open question: does Card B need more explicit instruction, or an example on the board?**
- **Absent authors** leave dead-end cards. Spares solve it.
- **The introvert problem is real** and half-room groups are the mitigation. If the room is small enough that halves are under ~8 people, don't split — the pool gets too easy and the activity ends in four minutes.
- Some students will not have read the project overview. The five-minute TA segment should stand alone well enough that they aren't lost, without rewarding not reading it.

**Clicker answer key**

B. Distractor A targets students who think `##` is the largest heading (confusing "level 2" with "biggest"). C targets students who don't know a line break separates list items. D targets students reading `##` as another bullet.

**Think It Over — assessment link**

- Q1 (Markdown vs. word processor) → Quiz 0, syllabus/intro content
- Q2 (what's in A but not B) → sets up the idea that a representation carries information the rendered form loses. Comes back in Data Representation and again in Digital Traces (Week 13).
- Q3 (why start here) → open; genuinely useful for the Digital Adulthood consolidation callback to computational thinking

- Whether to add "open Snap in a browser and confirm it loads" as a 60-second task at the end. It would de-risk Lab 2's tight Snap block considerably, and students have laptops-optional today. **Recommend considering it.**
